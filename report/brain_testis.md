# Conserved co-abundance modules: brain and testis

## Module 0

**Tissue pair**: brain and testis  
**Genes (30)**: ALDH16A1, AIFM1, ATP5F1A, ATP5F1B, ATP5F1C, ATXN2L, CS, CNOT1, ADAR, GGH, CAST, ME2, IMMT, MXRA7, PPP1R12A, PDCD4, PDP1, DCN, PITRM1, PSPC1, ATIC, UQCRC1, UQCRC2, SDHA, IARS2, SYNPO2, RARS1, ACAT1, TRAP1, TSPYL5

### Function

The primary evidence supports a mitochondrial energy-production module. The same six genes—ATP5F1A, ATP5F1B, ATP5F1C, SDHA, UQCRC1 and UQCRC2—occur in all globally corrected terms: respiratory chain complex (GO:CC respiratory chain complex; q_global = 0.0204), oxidative phosphorylation (KEGG Oxidative phosphorylation - Homo sapiens (human); q_global = 0.0304), and oxidative phosphorylation as a biological process (GO:BP oxidative phosphorylation; q_global = 0.0389). Their functions span ATP synthase, respiratory complex components, and succinate-linked electron transport. Supplementary STRING results independently connect the module to mitochondrial transit peptides (STRING Keyword Transit peptide; within-query FDR = 4.44e-05), the TCA cycle and respiratory electron transport (STRING RCTM The citric acid (TCA) cycle and respiratory electron transport; within-query FDR = 8.04e-05), the mitochondrial inner membrane (STRING Keyword Mitochondrion inner membrane; within-query FDR = 0.0023), electron-transport ATP synthesis (STRING RCTM Respiratory electron transport, ATP synthesis by chemiosmotic coupling, and heat production by uncoupling proteins.; within-query FDR = 0.0025), and several related mitochondrial annotations (6 supplementary STRING terms; within-query FDR 0.0061–0.0163). The remaining genes broaden this core toward mitochondrial translation, protein folding, cristae organization, proteolysis, pyruvate entry into metabolism, fatty-acid and acetyl-CoA metabolism, RNA processing, and cytoskeletal or extracellular functions. Thus, the best-supported interpretation is coordinated mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and respiratory metabolism, with associated mitochondrial maintenance and cellular stress functions. No other functional interpretation should be treated as globally established from the enrichment analysis.

**Cell composition.** This is not predominantly a plasma-protein, secreted-protein, or blood-cell module. HPA reports only a small secreted subset, while most genes have low immune-cell specificity; the two immune-cell-enriched examples are DCN and GGH, so blood contamination is not the leading explanation. The module is nevertheless composition-sensitive: many genes are tissue- or cell-type enhanced, especially in metabolically active tissues and cell types such as skeletal muscle, tongue, heart muscle, parietal cells, early or late spermatids, oocytes, choroid-plexus cells, and neuronal or glial populations. Most module proteins are detected in both brain and testis, supporting a shared mitochondrial program, although the absence of SYNPO2 protein detection in testis and the absence of ATXN2L protein detection in brain caution against treating every member as a universal component of the same cell population. The shared signal could therefore reflect both conserved mitochondrial biology and shared or overlapping metabolically active cell types.

### Novelty

The mitochondrial oxidative-phosphorylation interpretation is not novel at the gene-function level: the core genes have established annotations, STRING relationships, and hu.MAP memberships. The module may still represent a useful new observation at the level of conserved co-abundance across brain and testis, particularly because it combines a canonical respiratory core with peripheral genes that have weaker or absent STRING and hu.MAP support. However, absence from STRING or hu.MAP does not establish novelty, and the current evidence does not demonstrate a previously unknown physical complex or mechanism. A defensible conclusion is that this is a biologically coherent conserved co-abundance pattern, but not a demonstrably new mitochondrial pathway.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: in both brain and testis, metabolically demanding or developmentally active cell populations coordinate mitochondrial respiratory-chain capacity with ATP synthesis, TCA-cycle flux, mitochondrial translation, cristae organization, protein quality control, and stress-response pathways. The respiratory core may provide the energetic backbone, while IMMT, TRAP1, IARS2, PITRM1, AIFM1, PDP1 and related genes could help maintain inner-membrane architecture, mitochondrial proteostasis, translation, and response to respiratory stress. The non-mitochondrial members may reflect cell-state coupling, RNA or cytoskeletal regulation, or shared cellular composition rather than direct membership in one physical complex. This hypothesis requires validation using cell-resolved data, independent cohorts, and perturbation or metabolic assays.

## Module 1

**Tissue pair**: brain and testis  
**Genes (13)**: LRG1, DCTN1, QDPR, HDGFL3, AK1, ME3, ME1, RHOT1, PYGB, SEPTIN5, AARS1, ATP6V1A, RP2

### Function

This module is most consistent with a shared metabolic and intracellular logistics program rather than a narrowly defined pathway. ME1 and ME3 support malate-linked carbon metabolism and reducing-equivalent production; RHOT1 and DCTN1 support mitochondrial and microtubule-dependent transport; ATP6V1A supports vesicle and lysosome acidification; and AK1 supports adenine-nucleotide homeostasis. The network structure places ME3 at a central position and connects metabolic, transport, cytoskeletal, and organelle-related genes. No GO or KEGG term passed global correction, so no such term should be presented as significant. The module may reflect coordinated energy handling and organelle logistics, but it does not establish one unified biochemical pathway.

**Cell composition.** The module is not purely brain- or testis-specific. Most genes have low tissue specificity, and many are detected in both tissues. HPA associations include neurons, glia, Sertoli cells, spermatids, hepatocytes, ciliated cells, and immune-associated populations. Neutrophil-related signals recur among the cell-type and blood-cell annotations. LRG1 is liver enriched, associated with neutrophils, secreted to blood, and not detected in brain or testis by immunohistochemistry. QDPR is enriched in brain and liver and not detected in testis. Only one gene is secreted, so this is not predominantly a plasma-protein module. The findings raise the possibility of shared cell types, blood or immune-cell contribution, and broadly expressed housekeeping-like metabolism rather than a specialized program shared by all brain and testis cells.

**GO/KEGG enrichment**: 0 terms pass global BH (q_global < 0.05).

### Novelty

This should not be called a new pathway solely because most module edges are absent from STRING or hu.MAP. The ME1–ME3 relationship and the individual functions of several genes are established. The potentially novel observation is the cross-tissue co-abundance of this particular combination of metabolic, mitochondrial, trafficking, cytoskeletal, and cell-type-associated genes in brain and testis. That claim remains provisional because the network is sparse, no GO or KEGG term passed global correction, STRING returned no enrichment, and several genes suggest immune, hepatic, blood, or other cell-composition contributions. Replication with cell-composition adjustment and orthogonal protein or single-cell validation is needed.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects a shared demand for metabolic flexibility and organelle logistics in selected brain and testis cell populations. ME1 and ME3 may coordinate malate-linked carbon flux and redox metabolism, while RHOT1 and DCTN1 support mitochondrial and microtubule-dependent transport, ATP6V1A supports acidic vesicular compartments, and AK1 buffers adenine nucleotides. LRG1 and the immune- and liver-associated HPA signals may represent vascular, hepatic, circulating, or immune-cell contributions rather than part of a core brain–testis mechanism. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not an established causal model.

### Novelty

The module should not be presented as discovery of a new complex or a previously unknown oxidative-phosphorylation pathway. Its central complex-I structure is already strongly represented in STRING and hu.MAP, and the globally corrected GO/KEGG results reproduce well-established mitochondrial functions. A potentially novel aspect is the conservation of this particular co-abundance organization across brain and testis, including its peripheral connections to spermatogenic cell states, trafficking, cytoskeletal proteins, mitochondrial lipid and amino-acid metabolism, and mitochondrial membrane organization. However, the fact sheet does not provide an external benchmark of previously reported brain-testis modules, so genuine novelty of the cross-tissue pattern cannot be established conclusively. Absence of some peripheral genes from STRING or hu.MAP is evidence of limited prior annotation, not proof that their relationships are novel.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: specialized brain and testis cells may preserve a shared mitochondrial resource-allocation program in which complex-I electron transfer and proton-gradient generation are coordinated with mitochondrial membrane organization, protein import, respiratory-complex assembly, and nutrient or lipid metabolism. In testis, this program may support the energetic demands of spermatogenic differentiation; in brain, it may support metabolically demanding neuronal, glial, or choroid-plexus cells. The co-abundance links to CHCHD3, TIMM50, LYRM7, CISD1, IDH3A, OXSM, and ECI1 suggest coupling between respiratory output, cristae organization, mitochondrial protein handling, redox control, and substrate supply. This is a mechanistic model generated from the co-abundance and annotation evidence, not proof of direct regulation or physical interaction among all module members.

### Novelty

The module should not be described as a newly discovered molecular function. Its principal components and relationships are already well established in STRING and hu.MAP, especially the spliceosome, CCT chaperonin, translation-initiation, aminoacylation, RNA helicase, and RNA-surveillance systems. The potentially novel aspect is the tissue-pair co-abundance organization: these known components appear together as a conserved brain-testis module, with links across otherwise distinct RNA-processing, translation, folding, transport, and surveillance subsystems. That organization could be a new systems-level observation, but the supplied evidence does not establish novelty by itself. In particular, missing STRING or hu.MAP links cannot prove that an association is novel. Validation would require replication across independent cohorts, preservation of the module in separately processed tissue data, and testing whether its abundance tracks specific neuronal, germ-cell, or differentiation states.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: this module coordinates post-transcriptional gene expression in cells with demanding RNA and protein-production requirements, such as neural cells and differentiating germ cells. A possible mechanism is that spliceosomal and RNA-helicase components generate correctly processed transcripts; SRP, translation-initiation, and aminoacyl-tRNA-synthetase components support their translation; CCT chaperonins fold newly synthesized proteins; and UPF1, XRN2, CAPRIN1, and related factors remove or localize selected transcripts. The CCT-associated telomerase and reproductive annotations may reflect a shared proliferative or germ-cell state rather than a dedicated sperm-recognition pathway. This is a mechanistic model, not a demonstrated causal chain; the co-abundance data do not establish directionality or direct regulation.

### Function

The module is best interpreted as a composite cellular maintenance and proliferative program, with a particularly strong nuclear component. Primary enrichment supports nucleic-acid binding (GO:MF nucleic acid binding; q_global = 0.00948), protein-DNA complexes (GO:CC protein-DNA complex; q_global = 0.0107), cadherin binding (GO:MF cadherin binding; q_global = 0.0204), and mitotic DNA replication (GO:BP mitotic DNA replication; q_global = 0.0294). **Cell composition.** This is not predominantly a plasma or secreted-protein module: the HPA summary identifies a minority of secreted genes, while most proteins are intracellular or membrane-associated. The module should therefore not be assumed to represent a neuron-specific and germ-cell-specific program. The testis-associated replication and spermatogenic annotations for MCM genes, NASP, GMPS, and related genes provide biological support for a genuine testicular proliferative component, but the HPA evidence also supports a broader cell-state explanation.

### Novelty

The module should not be claimed as an entirely new molecular pathway. Its principal components are already well established: MCM2–MCM4–MCM6 represent replication machinery, PRKDC–XRCC6 represents DNA double-strand-break repair, HNRNPH1–HNRNPK–PTBP1 represents RNA-binding and processing biology, and the FLNA–FLNB–TLN1–VCL–IQGAP1 group represents cytoskeletal adhesion. The potentially novel aspect is the tissue-pair co-abundance organization that places these known systems in one conserved brain–testis module, together with metabolic, vesicle, and stress-response genes. That is a systems-level observation requiring validation, not proof of a new biochemical complex. Lack of a complete matching STRING or hu.MAP complex would not establish novelty, because co-abundance can arise from shared cell composition, coordinated regulation, indirect functional coupling, or incompletely catalogued interactions. The strongest novelty claim is therefore a conserved cross-tissue expression architecture linking replication and chromatin biology with adhesion and cellular maintenance.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents a shared high-demand cell-state program in brain and testis in which proliferating or differentiating cells coordinate genome replication and chromatin handling with RNA processing, protein synthesis, vesicle trafficking, redox and metabolic support, and cytoskeletal adhesion. In testis, this could support spermatogonial and spermatocyte proliferation and spermatid differentiation; in brain, it could reflect vascular, glial, progenitor, or other non-neuronal cellular contributions rather than mature neurons alone. The MCM and histone-associated genes may define the nuclear replication component, while the annexin–filamin–talin–vinculin–IQGAP1 group may stabilize cell contacts and force transmission. Shared blood, vascular, and platelet-associated signatures could contribute to the apparent conservation. This model predicts that cell-type-resolved analysis and removal or adjustment for vascular and blood-derived signals will determine whether the module persists within specific brain and testis cell populations.
