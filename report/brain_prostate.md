### Function

This is a conserved nuclear gene-expression and RNA-processing module. The strongest primary evidence is for nucleic-acid binding (GO:MF nucleic acid binding; q_global = 1.41e-05), mRNA processing (GO:BP mRNA processing; q_global = 0.000237), gene expression and macromolecule biosynthesis (GO:BP macromolecule biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.000435) (GO:BP gene expression; q_global = 0.000494), nuclear speck localization (GO:CC nuclear speck; q_global = 0.00077), RNA metabolism (GO:BP mRNA metabolic process; q_global = 0.00094) (GO:BP RNA metabolic process; q_global = 0.00293) (GO:BP nucleic acid metabolic process; q_global = 0.00293), ribonucleoprotein complexes and nuclear ribonucleoprotein granules (GO:CC ribonucleoprotein complex; q_global = 0.00115) (GO:CC nuclear ribonucleoprotein granule; q_global = 0.00313) (GO:CC ribonucleoprotein granule; q_global = 0.039), RNA biosynthesis (GO:BP nucleic acid biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.00191) (GO:BP RNA biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.00356), DNA binding (GO:MF DNA binding; q_global = 0.00403), and spliceosome-related processes (9 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 0.00613–0.0354; see the table). The convergence of these independent globally corrected terms supports coordinated control of pre-mRNA recognition, spliceosome assembly, RNA splicing, RNA maturation, transcription, and related nuclear organization. The network also contains chromatin and transcriptional regulators, nuclear-envelope components, DNA-repair proteins, nucleotide-biosynthesis enzymes, and translation-associated proteins, indicating that the module is broader than a single spliceosomal complex. STRING provides supplementary support for nucleoplasm, nuclear lumen, nuclear body, nuclear speck, capped pre-mRNA processing, and major-pathway mRNA splicing (7 supplementary STRING terms; within-query FDR 8.55e-09–0.00059), but these are supplementary rather than globally corrected enrichment evidence.

**Cell composition.** The module is not dominated by plasma proteins, secreted proteins, or blood-cell markers. HPA identifies only a single secreted gene, and the genes generally have low immune-cell specificity, arguing against a blood-derived explanation for the shared brain-prostate signal. Most annotated genes have low tissue specificity and are detected at the protein level in brain and, for most genes, prostate, which is consistent with broadly expressed cellular machinery. The module is therefore substantially housekeeping-like, particularly for nuclear RNA processing and gene expression, although it includes cell-type-enhanced genes in spermatogenic, neural, glial, progenitor, muscle, and epithelial contexts. The spermatid and primary-spermatocyte enrichments may be relevant to prostate biology, but they do not by themselves establish a prostate-specific mechanism. HPA detection supports protein presence in both tissues, while the absence of prostate detection for selected genes such as NUP93, RUVBL2, and BZW2 cautions against assuming uniform protein-level deployment across every prostate cell type.

### Novelty

The molecular functions represented here are established, and much of the internal connectivity is already supported by STRING and hu.MAP. Therefore this should not be presented as discovery of a new spliceosome, RNA helicase function, or chromatin complex. The potentially novel finding is the reproducible co-abundance module shared across brain and prostate and the way it joins spliceosomal, transcriptional, chromatin, nuclear-envelope, and nucleotide-metabolism components in a cross-tissue context. That claim remains a systems-level observation rather than proof of a new physical complex or tissue-specific mechanism. The absence of some genes from STRING or hu.MAP is compatible with incomplete coverage and does not establish novelty. Confirmation would require independent cohorts, preservation testing, cell-composition adjustment, and perturbation or biochemical experiments.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the shared module reflects a conserved nuclear gene-expression state in which transcriptional activity, co-transcriptional pre-mRNA processing, spliceosome assembly, RNA quality control, and chromatin organization are coordinately regulated. SF3A1, DDX46, DDX42, and SNRPA1 could form a core U2-spliceosomal axis; DDX5, FUS, HNRNPH1, RBM39, TCERG1, and SRRT could couple RNA remodeling and splice-site choice to transcriptional regulation; and CHD4-MTA2, SUPT16H, POLR2B, LMNB1, and TMPO could connect this process to chromatin, elongation, and nuclear architecture. CAPRIN1 and the FUS-associated RNA-granule context could provide a bridge to RNA storage or stress-responsive RNA handling. This is a mechanistic model, not a demonstrated physical assembly. Its tissue sharing may arise from common cellular requirements, shared cell states, or shared cell-type composition rather than a brain-prostate-specific pathway.

### Novelty

The individual relationships and complexes are not, by themselves, a new discovery: many are directly supported by STRING and hu.MAP, and the genes encode well-established components of translation, chaperoning, RNA metabolism, purine synthesis and proteostasis. The potentially novel aspect is the reproducible co-abundance configuration shared by brain and prostate, particularly the way these established machines are coordinated in the same cross-tissue module. However, the fact sheet does not provide an external catalogue comparison, replication cohort, perturbation experiment or tissue-specific validation sufficient to establish that the module is a new biological entity. Absence from a particular database would not prove novelty. The conservative conclusion is that this is a potentially informative cross-tissue network-level observation built largely from known biology, rather than evidence for a previously unknown molecular complex.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents a conserved cellular capacity program that coordinates translation with the handling of newly synthesized proteins. Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and translation-initiation factors may supply accurately charged tRNAs and productive translation; SRP components may route a subset of nascent proteins to the endoplasmic reticulum; CCT and HSPH1 may fold or stabilize proteins; RNA surveillance, splicing and decay factors may regulate transcript quality; and proteasome components may remove damaged or surplus proteins. Purine and one-carbon metabolic enzymes may support the nucleotide and energy demands of this process. In brain and prostate, the shared module could therefore reflect common requirements for sustained protein synthesis, proteostasis and RNA quality control. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal pathway; testing would require cell-type-resolved data and perturbation of representative hubs.

### Novelty

The molecular function is not a new biological process: DNA replication, the MCM machinery, PCNA-dependent synthesis, FEN1-mediated processing, chromatin organization, and chromosome control are established biology and are supported by STRING, hu.MAP, gene annotations, and substantial literature. The potentially novel result is the identification of this particular co-abundance module as conserved between brain and prostate. The fact sheet does not provide an external catalogue of prior cross-tissue modules, so novelty cannot be claimed as established. Absence of a STRING or hu.MAP relationship would not prove novelty, and several relationships are already known. A defensible conclusion is that this is a potentially informative cross-tissue replication-state module, with novelty at the network or tissue-comparison level still requiring comparison against prior co-abundance studies and independent cohorts.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the shared brain–prostate module represents a conserved abundance program of cycling, progenitor-like, or otherwise replication-competent cells. In this model, the MCM machinery licenses and initiates replication; PCNA and RFC3 support processive DNA synthesis; FEN1 resolves replication intermediates; NASP and the chromatin-associated proteins support histone handling and chromatin restoration; RCC1, SMC2, STAG2, and MAD2L1 coordinate chromosome organization, condensation, cohesion, and segregation. The cell-type patterns in HPA are consistent with this interpretation, but they also raise an alternative hypothesis that the shared module is driven by similar proportions of progenitor or proliferative cells in the two tissue sample groups. This should be tested with cell-resolved expression, proliferation markers, histology, and replication-state measurements.

### Novelty

The module should not be claimed as a newly discovered mitochondrial pathway or a new complex. Its central biology is already well established by the repeated STRING relationships, the recurring hu.MAP complex-I models (hu.MAP huMAP3_14018.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_06397.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09982.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09567.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14878.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_02819.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_07653.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_08132.1), the pyruvate-dehydrogenase-related complexes (hu.MAP huMAP3_12248.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_07213.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_04919.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_10702.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_08216.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_07514.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09761.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_10899.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_10412.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14339.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_11877.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14864.1), and the extensive functional annotations (71 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 5.2e-09–0.0477; see the table). What may be novel is the observation that this particular combination of respiratory-chain, mitochondrial matrix, amino-acid-catabolic, redox, and mitochondrial-maintenance genes forms a conserved co-abundance module across brain and prostate. That cross-tissue organization and its exact peripheral membership could represent a new systems-level observation, but novelty requires comparison with the originating co-abundance datasets, published tissue modules, and independent cohorts. The presence of genes lacking strong STRING or hu.MAP support, such as HDDC3, RMDN3, or some peripheral metabolic and trafficking genes, makes the module potentially informative beyond a simple catalogued complex, but absence from those resources alone does not establish novelty.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the conserved module represents a coordinated mitochondrial fitness program that couples complex-I abundance and assembly to carbon delivery, branched-chain and organic-acid catabolism, redox protection, and mitochondrial genome or RNA maintenance. In this model, variation in the complex-I core changes respiratory electron flow and proton-motive-force generation; PC, PDHB, DLAT, DBT, and related enzymes regulate entry of carbon into central metabolism; TXNRD2 and other oxidoreductases buffer mitochondrial redox stress; and TFAM, TRNT1, NIPSNAP1, and NIPSNAP2 help maintain mitochondrial gene expression, organelle quality, or turnover. The shared brain–prostate signal could therefore arise because both tissues contain cell populations that require sustained oxidative phosphorylation, while the exact module amplitude may also reflect differences in cell composition, nutrient state, or mitochondrial stress. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not proof of direct regulation or of a disease-specific process.

## Module 6

### Function

The module is most consistent with coordinated intracellular energy and biosynthetic metabolism centered on mitochondria, with a possible peroxisomal contribution. Its genes cover mitochondrial translation through TUFM, de novo pyrimidine synthesis through DHODH, mitochondrial electron transfer and steroid-related metabolism through FDXR, the TCA cycle through FH, peroxisomal fatty-acid beta-oxidation through ACAA1, and predicted mitochondrial glycerophospholipid biology through HDHD5. TUFM is the central network connector, linking DHODH, FH, and HDHD5, while the remaining genes form peripheral links. The module therefore appears to represent a coordinated metabolic state rather than a narrowly defined canonical pathway.

**Cell composition.** This is not a plasma-protein or secreted-protein module: none of the genes is annotated as secreted, and all have low immune-cell specificity in HPA. The genes are predominantly intracellular, with mitochondrial localization for TUFM, DHODH, FDXR, FH, and HDHD5, and peroxisomal localization for ACAA1. HPA shows heterogeneous tissue enrichment, including liver, adrenal gland, and kidney, and cell-type enhancement across hepatocytes, adrenal cells, epithelial cells, and germ-cell populations. All genes have cell-type-enhanced annotations, but there is no single dominant shared cell type. Protein detection is generally present in brain and prostate, although ACAA1 is not detected in prostate and FDXR is not detected in either reported tissue. Thus, the shared module is unlikely to be explained primarily by blood contamination or secretion; it may instead reflect conserved metabolic programs, cell-composition effects, or tissue-state effects that require experimental validation.

### Novelty

The module cannot be declared a wholly new biological pathway. DHODH-FDXR is already supported by STRING, and FDXR-HDHD5 occurs within hu.MAP complex C1. Nevertheless, the particular conserved co-abundance combination across brain and prostate may be a potentially novel observation because most module relationships are not represented by the supplied interaction resources. Absence from STRING or hu.MAP does not establish novelty: it may reflect incomplete interaction coverage, context dependence, indirect regulation, or co-abundance without physical binding. The strongest defensible conclusion is that this is a potentially novel cross-tissue co-abundance module built around partly known mitochondrial and metabolic biology, requiring replication and targeted validation before being described as a new mechanism.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects a conserved organelle-metabolic program in which mitochondrial translation capacity through TUFM supports respiratory and biosynthetic activity, DHODH couples pyrimidine production to the mitochondrial electron-transfer environment, FDXR supplies reductive support for mitochondrial electron-transfer and steroid-related reactions, FH maintains TCA-cycle flux, and ACAA1 and HDHD5 extend the program toward lipid and membrane metabolism. The shared abundance pattern in brain and prostate may therefore arise when cells in both tissues require coordinated mitochondrial maintenance, nucleotide production, and lipid handling. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not an established causal pathway; the sparse known interaction network, lack of globally significant GO or KEGG enrichment, and heterogeneous HPA cell-type annotations mean that shared cell composition or a broader metabolic state remain plausible alternatives.

### Novelty

The conserved brain-prostate co-abundance module may be a new systems-level observation, because the fact sheet identifies a cross-tissue co-abundance pattern rather than a previously established single complex. However, it should not be described as a newly discovered molecular pathway. Much of its internal biology is already known: STRING and hu.MAP recover RNA-binding, RNA-export, rRNA-processing, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, and proteasome machinery. The absence of a particular edge from STRING or hu.MAP would not demonstrate novelty, because those resources are incomplete and the co-abundance relationship may be indirect, context-dependent, or driven by shared cell states. The defensible novelty claim is therefore the conserved coordination of several established nuclear RNA, ribosome, translation-support, and proteostasis components across brain and prostate, pending replication in independent cohorts and experimental testing.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents a conserved cellular state in which increased nuclear RNA surveillance, processing, export and ribosome biogenesis are coordinated with translation capacity and proteasomal quality control. DDX21, WDR43 and the nucleolar machinery could coordinate rRNA maturation; ADAR, EFTUD2 and associated RNA-binding proteins could shape RNA processing and export; ribosomal and aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase components could support protein synthesis; and the proteasome subset could remove damaged or misfolded proteins. The ossification and osteoblast annotations may reflect a shared differentiation or stress-response program rather than literal bone formation in either tissue. This hypothesis predicts coordinated changes in RNA-processing activity, nucleolar function, translation, and proteostasis, but co-abundance alone does not establish directionality or physical interaction.
