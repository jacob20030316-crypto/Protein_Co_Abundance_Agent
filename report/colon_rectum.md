## Module 0

**Tissue pair**: colon and rectum

### Function

The module is a conserved nuclear RNA-metabolism and ribonucleoprotein module shared by colon and rectum. The strongest primary evidence supports RNA processing (GO:BP RNA processing; q_global = 3.94e-06), broad nucleic-acid and RNA binding (GO:MF nucleic acid binding; q_global = 2.61e-05)(GO:MF RNA binding; q_global = 4.39e-05), nuclear and nucleolar localization (GO:CC nuclear lumen; q_global = 0.000454)(GO:CC nucleolus; q_global = 0.0023), RNA biosynthesis and metabolism (GO:BP RNA biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.000739)(GO:BP nucleic acid biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.00105)(GO:BP RNA metabolic process; q_global = 0.00229)(GO:BP nucleobase-containing compound biosynthetic process; q_global = 0.00623)(GO:BP nucleic acid metabolic process; q_global = 0.00822), ribonucleoprotein-complex biogenesis (GO:CC ribonucleoprotein complex; q_global = 0.00151)(GO:BP ribonucleoprotein complex biogenesis; q_global = 0.00167), pre-ribosome and ribosome biogenesis (GO:BP rRNA processing; q_global = 0.011)(GO:CC preribosome; q_global = 0.0145)(GO:BP ribosome biogenesis; q_global = 0.0437), and pre-mRNA splicing (GO:BP RNA splicing, via transesterification reactions; q_global = 0.00174)(GO:BP mRNA splicing, via spliceosome; q_global = 0.00174)(GO:BP RNA splicing; q_global = 0.0126). The presence of EFTUD2, SNRNP200 and SNU13 further points to the U4/U6-U5 tri-snRNP and spliceosome (GO:CC U4/U6 x U5 tri-snRNP complex; q_global = 0.033)(GO:CC spliceosomal tri-snRNP complex; q_global = 0.033), while NOP56, PDCD11, RRS1, DDX21 and SNU13 support nucleolar rRNA maturation and ribosome assembly (GO:BP rRNA metabolic process; q_global = 0.00131)(GO:BP rRNA processing; q_global = 0.011)(GO:CC preribosome; q_global = 0.0145)(GO:MF snoRNA binding; q_global = 0.0434)(GO:BP ribosome biogenesis; q_global = 0.0437). MACROH2A1 may connect this RNA-centered module to chromatin regulation, but the principal interpretation is coordinated nuclear RNA processing, splicing and ribosome biogenesis rather than a tissue-specific metabolic pathway.

**Cell composition.** The module is not composed of plasma or secreted proteins: HPA reports no secreted genes. It is also not blood-cell enriched according to the low immune-cell specificity of every gene, and it is not dominated by a strongly tissue-restricted cell population. All genes have low tissue specificity, and every protein is detected in both colon and rectum. Several genes have enhanced expression in selected cell types, including keratinocyte, germ-cell, erythrocyte-progenitor, megakaryocyte-erythroid-progenitor and monocyte-progenitor populations, but these signals are dispersed rather than forming a coherent blood signature. The shared module therefore more plausibly reflects a broadly expressed nuclear housekeeping program, with possible contributions from common epithelial or proliferative cell states, than blood contamination.

### Novelty

This is unlikely to be a newly discovered molecular machinery, because its central functions and several relationships are already established by primary GO evidence, STRING associations and hu.MAP complexes. The potentially novel result is the conserved co-abundance behavior of this particular combination across colon and rectum, especially the coupling of spliceosomal, nucleolar and broader nuclear RNA-processing genes in these tissues. That claim remains a systems-level observation rather than discovery of a new complex or pathway. Lack of hu.MAP membership for genes such as RSL1D1, NOP56, RRS1, PDCD11, DDX21, ACIN1, CPSF2, MACROH2A1 and DHX9 does not establish novelty, because absence from a resource can reflect incomplete coverage, context dependence or complex definitions. The failed web search yielded no usable source supporting a stronger novelty claim.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: in colon and rectum, the module represents a coordinated demand-responsive nuclear RNA-production program in which nucleolar ribosome biogenesis and pre-mRNA splicing are co-regulated to support epithelial maintenance, cell renewal or stress adaptation. The nucleolar branch, involving DDX21, NOP56, PDCD11, RRS1, RSL1D1 and SNU13, could coordinate rRNA maturation and ribosome assembly, while the EFTUD2-SNRNP200-SNU13 branch could support spliceosomal tri-snRNP function. DHX9, HNRNPR, MATR3, ACIN1 and CPSF2 could connect RNA remodeling, RNA binding, exon-junction-associated processing and transcript-end maturation. This is a mechanistic model inferred from co-abundance and annotations, not evidence of direct regulation or physical assembly of the entire module.

### Novelty

The biological mechanism represented by the central subgraph is not a new discovery: MCM-complex membership, DNA replication, helicase activity and RFC-associated replication functions are already established by the primary annotations, STRING relationships and hu.MAP complexes. What may be novel is the observation that these genes form a conserved co-abundance module shared by colon and rectum, particularly with the accompanying RNA-processing and translation-linked peripheral genes. That tissue-pair-specific co-abundance pattern cannot be established as novel from database absence alone. The appropriate conclusion is that this is a potentially novel tissue-network observation built around a well-known replication machinery, requiring comparison with published colon–rectum co-expression studies and independent cohorts before claiming a new biological mechanism.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects coordinated abundance of replication-competent or replication-stressed cells present in both colon and rectum. The MCM core may support origin licensing, fork progression and repair-associated replication, with RFC4 contributing to processive DNA synthesis and checkpoint-linked replication control. The association of DDX46, NSUN2, UPF1, XRN2 and EIF4G1 suggests that RNA maturation, RNA surveillance and translation capacity may be co-regulated with replication demand. The progenitor-associated HPA pattern makes a shared proliferative or progenitor-like cell state plausible, but the co-abundance data do not by themselves establish cell lineage, replication stress, causality or physical interaction among every module member.

### Novelty

The biological mechanism itself is not a new discovery: the roles of desmin, filamin C, vinculin, myosin light-chain kinase, leiomodin, synemin, and related proteins in muscle cytoskeleton, contractility, and adhesion are established by the annotations and disease literature in the fact sheet. However, the available evidence does not establish that this exact module or tissue-pair pattern is novel. Lack of a STRING or hu.MAP representation would not prove novelty, and the presence of many known interactions argues for a conserved biological program rather than a newly discovered molecular complex. A claim of novelty would require comparison with published colon and rectum co-abundance modules and independent replication.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects coordinated abundance of contractile stromal and vascular smooth-muscle cells that mechanically couple the intestinal wall and its vasculature to extracellular-matrix adhesion sites. ### Novelty

The module should not be described as a newly discovered molecular pathway. Its central biology is already well established by the complement and coagulation annotations, strong STRING relationships, and hu.MAP complexes. What may be novel is the observation that these genes form a conserved co-abundance module in the colon–rectum tissue dataset, particularly if this preservation has not previously been reported in that dataset or analytical framework. However, the fact sheet does not provide an external module atlas or an independent replication, so the novelty of the tissue-level module cannot be established conclusively. Database representation supports biological credibility but does not by itself establish discovery novelty.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the conserved colon–rectum signal is driven mainly by a shared circulating plasma-protein environment. Liver-derived C1R and C1S may provide classical complement proteolytic activity, CFH may restrain complement amplification, and the PLG–KNG1 axis may couple fibrinolysis and the kallikrein–kinin system to local vascular or tissue injury responses. Blood microparticles, plasma leakage, or vascular proximity could make this coordinated extracellular system appear in both tissue groups. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not proof of local complement activation in colon or rectum; testing it would require independent measurements of plasma contamination, vascular content, local transcript or protein production, and complement or fibrinolytic activity.

### Novelty

The module should not be claimed as a wholly new biological pathway or protein complex. Its genes, functions, STRING relationships, literature associations, and hu.MAP assemblies are already well established, and the complete co-occurrence is strongly recognizable as a neutrophil granule program. A potentially novel aspect could be the discovery that this particular compact abundance pattern is conserved across colon and rectum, especially if the exact tissue-pair module and its quantitative behavior have not previously been reported. However, that claim requires comparison with the source study, prior tissue co-abundance networks, and independent cohorts. Absence from a particular hu.MAP complex, especially for AZU1, is not evidence of novelty. The defensible conclusion is that the biology is established, while the tissue-pair network-level reproducibility may be a new observation requiring external validation.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: shared neutrophil or myeloid infiltration in colon and rectum generates a coordinated extracellular antimicrobial signature. Neutrophil granules containing MPO, ELANE, CTSG, AZU1, and LTF may be released during mucosal immune surveillance or inflammation; MPO could contribute oxidant-mediated microbial killing, the proteases could promote microbial and matrix proteolysis, and LTF could provide iron-sequestering and antimicrobial activity. Their coordinated abundance may therefore mark neutrophil degranulation and antimicrobial activity in the sampled tissues. This is a mechanistic hypothesis rather than proof of active degranulation, because co-abundance and tissue-level HPA absence do not establish cellular localization, directionality, or causality.

### Novelty

The molecular function is not novel: collagen, basement-membrane, laminin, nidogen, proteoglycan, and fibrillin relationships are established by the strong STRING network, the hu.MAP memberships, the gene descriptions, and the disease literature. The potentially novel observation is the conserved co-abundance behavior of this particular extracellular-matrix combination across colon and rectum. The fact sheet does not provide a comparison with previously published colon–rectum modules, independent cohorts, or prior co-abundance studies, so it cannot establish that the module itself is a new discovery. Absence of some genes from hu.MAP or absence of a STRING interaction for particular pairs would not prove novelty. The appropriate conclusion is that this is a biologically credible, potentially useful tissue-pair module whose module-level novelty requires comparison with prior datasets and replication.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents coordinated production and organization of a stromal extracellular scaffold in the shared connective-tissue and vascular compartments of colon and rectum. COL1A1 and COL1A2 may provide fibrillar tensile material, COL6A1, COL6A2, and COL6A3 may organize collagen-VI microfibrillar structure, and FBN1, EMILIN1, and BGN may link fibrillar and elastic-associated matrix components. HSPG2, LAMA4, NID1, and NID2 may assemble or stabilize basement membranes and couple the matrix to cell-adhesion and integrin signaling. The co-abundance pattern could therefore reflect coordinated fibroblast, pericyte, or vascular-stromal activity. This is a mechanistic hypothesis rather than a demonstrated causal model; it should be tested with cell-resolved data, spatial localization, matrix-protein measurements, and perturbation of matrix assembly or integrin signaling.
