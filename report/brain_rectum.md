# Conserved co-abundance modules: brain and rectum

## Module 0

**Tissue pair**: brain and rectum  
**Genes (5)**: SLC3A2, SLC1A5, CANX, QTRT2, VDAC2

### Function

The module most plausibly reflects coordinated amino-acid transport, metabolic exchange, and cellular stress handling rather than a single established pathway. The strongest functional signal is the SLC1A5–SLC3A2 transporter pair, supported by the supplementary STRING terms for amino-acid transport, transport across the plasma membrane, and the cyclosporin-A response pathway (STRING WikiPathways mRNA, protein, and metabolite inducation pathway by cyclosporin A; within-query FDR = 0.0113), (STRING Keyword Amino-acid transport; within-query FDR = 0.037), (STRING RCTM Amino acid transport across the plasma membrane; within-query FDR = 0.0469). VDAC2 adds a mitochondrial outer-membrane and anion-transport component, with supplementary support from ferroptosis and anion-transporter terms (STRING WikiPathways Ferroptosis; within-query FDR = 0.0113), (STRING Function Anion transmembrane transporter activity; within-query FDR = 0.025). CANX contributes endoplasmic-reticulum protein folding and quality control, while QTRT2 contributes tRNA queuosine modification. No GO or KEGG term passed global correction, so these annotations should be interpreted as functional context rather than statistically established enrichment. The network is coherent at the level of membrane transport, organelle homeostasis, and stress adaptation, but it does not establish one specific biological process.

**Cell composition.** This is not a module dominated by secreted or plasma proteins: the HPA summary reports no secreted genes. All module genes have low tissue specificity, and the proteins are detected in brain; most are also detected in rectum, with SLC3A2 being the exception in the supplied HPA data. The module is therefore broadly expressed rather than tissue-specific. Most genes have low immune-cell specificity, although SLC1A5 is immune-cell enhanced in plasmacytoid dendritic cells. The HPA cell-type annotations are dispersed across several specialized cell types rather than concentrated in one shared lineage. These observations argue against a simple blood-cell or circulating-protein explanation, while leaving open the possibility that shared broadly expressed cell types or common metabolic states contribute to the brain–rectum co-abundance pattern.

### Novelty

The module should not be claimed as a wholly new molecular pathway. The SLC1A5–SLC3A2 relationship is already represented in STRING, and the functions of the individual proteins are established. The potentially novel aspect is the conserved co-abundance arrangement across brain and rectum that brings amino-acid transporters together with VDAC2, CANX, and QTRT2. However, the absence of a hu.MAP complex or additional STRING interactions does not by itself prove novelty. Because no GO or KEGG term passed global correction and the network is small, the finding is best regarded as a candidate cross-tissue regulatory or cellular-state module requiring independent validation.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module may represent a shared cellular adaptation to nutrient demand and proteostatic or metabolic stress. SLC1A5 and SLC3A2 could coordinate neutral amino-acid uptake, particularly glutamine and other amino acids, while VDAC2 could couple metabolite exchange at the mitochondrial outer membrane with stress or apoptotic signaling. CANX could support folding and quality control for membrane and secretory-pathway proteins, and QTRT2 could reflect accompanying translational or tRNA-modification demands. In both tissues, a common metabolic or proteostatic state could therefore produce the observed co-abundance pattern. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not an established causal pathway from the supplied evidence.

## Module 1

**Tissue pair**: brain and rectum

### Function

The module is best interpreted as a circulating plasma protease, complement, coagulation, hemostasis, and wound-response program. The strongest primary evidence is the blood-microparticle signal (GO:CC blood microparticle; q_global = 1.45e-06), accompanied by globally significant complement and coagulation cascades (KEGG Complement and coagulation cascades - Homo sapiens (human); q_global = 3.4e-05), heparin binding (GO:MF heparin binding; q_global = 0.00489), regulation of coagulation and hemostasis (GO:BP regulation of blood coagulation; q_global = 0.00682) (GO:BP regulation of hemostasis; q_global = 0.00682) (GO:BP regulation of coagulation; q_global = 0.0105), proteolysis (GO:BP proteolysis; q_global = 0.00876), wound-response regulation (GO:BP regulation of wound healing; q_global = 0.0286) (GO:BP regulation of response to wounding; q_global = 0.0343), glycosaminoglycan binding (GO:MF glycosaminoglycan binding; q_global = 0.0343), serine-type endopeptidase activity (GO:MF serine-type endopeptidase activity; q_global = 0.0367), and humoral immunity (GO:BP humoral immune response; q_global = 0.0406). The external-stimulus and response-to-stimulus terms (GO:BP response to external stimulus; q_global = 0.0179) (GO:BP regulation of response to stimulus; q_global = 0.0336) are consistent with a coordinated extracellular defense and injury-response milieu. The biology is therefore more consistent with a shared blood-derived extracellular module than with a brain-specific or rectum-specific intracellular pathway.

**Cell composition.** This is overwhelmingly a liver-derived, secreted plasma-protein signature. HPA identifies liver enrichment for every module gene, hepatocyte enrichment for most of the module, and secretion to blood for most of the genes. The proteins were not detected by HPA in the surveyed brain or rectum samples, and nearly all genes were not detected in immune cells. This argues against a conventional resident immune-cell module and strongly raises the possibility that the shared brain–rectum signal reflects blood, vascular content, plasma carryover, or a common sampled extracellular compartment. The HPA cell-type signals for podocytes, fibroblasts, choroid-plexus epithelial cells, and stromal cells may indicate additional sites of production or uptake, but they do not outweigh the coherent liver and plasma pattern. CP and HPX also connect the module to metal and heme handling, while A1BG is a plasma glycoprotein with comparatively uncertain molecular function.

### Novelty

The exact conserved brain–rectum co-abundance module may be a new analytical observation, but the underlying biology is not novel on the supplied evidence. Its genes, functions, STRING links, and hu.MAP complexes largely recapitulate established plasma complement, coagulation, fibrinolysis, heme-transport, and protease-inhibitor biology. The cross-tissue conservation is potentially interesting as a reproducible blood-derived signature, yet it cannot by itself demonstrate a new brain–rectum mechanism. Absence of a matching module in STRING or hu.MAP would not prove novelty, and the provided resources instead show extensive prior knowledge. A defensible novelty claim would require comparison with published co-abundance modules, the source cohort, sample-quality covariates, and independent tissue datasets.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the conserved signal is generated primarily by coordinated abundance of circulating liver-derived proteins in vascular or plasma-associated material captured in both tissue groups. Within that extracellular milieu, complement proteases and regulators could mediate innate defense, SERPINC1 and APOH could constrain or modulate coagulation, PLG and KNG1 could connect fibrinolysis with kinin generation, and HPX and CP could buffer heme, iron, and oxidative stress. This is a mechanistic interpretation of the network and annotations, not proof of active complement or coagulation inside brain or rectum. The lack of HPA protein detection in both tissues makes vascular carryover, local extracellular scarcity, or assay-related plasma contribution important alternative explanations that should be tested by matched plasma measurements, vascular-content markers, histology, and tissue-localized protein assays.

### Function

The module is best interpreted as a contractile-cell and cell-matrix adhesion program. The primary evidence is exceptionally coherent: contractile muscle fiber, myofibril, sarcomere, I band, Z disc, actin binding, actin cytoskeleton, focal adhesion, cell-substrate junction, muscle contraction, integrin-mediated signaling, extracellular matrix, basement membrane, and cell-matrix adhesion all pass global correction (46 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 3.19e-11–0.0428; see the table). The convergent biology is an actomyosin apparatus mechanically coupled to integrin adhesions and basement-membrane proteins. The module therefore likely reflects structural force generation, transmission, and sensing in smooth-muscle, vascular smooth-muscle, pericyte, or related contractile cells rather than a brain-specific neuronal process. The tissue-development terms (6 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 0.00888–0.0205; see the table) are compatible with maintenance or remodeling of these cells, but do not by themselves establish a developmental mechanism.

**Cell composition.** HPA strongly supports a contractile vascular and smooth-muscle composition. ### Novelty

The broad biological interpretation is not a new discovery: contractile fibers, focal adhesions, integrin-linked kinase signaling, basement membrane, and actin machinery are well established, and many module edges are already represented in STRING. The module may still be a novel systems-level observation because it identifies a conserved co-abundance neighborhood across brain and rectum that combines smooth-muscle contractile machinery, vascular or stromal matrix, and adhesion components. Its novelty would therefore reside in the tissue-pair co-abundance organization, relative coordination, and possible cell-composition signal, not in discovering previously unknown functions or protein interactions. The absence of hu.MAP support for many genes does not establish novelty, since hu.MAP only reports particular complexes and several partial complexes are present. A stronger novelty claim would require comparison with independent brain and rectum cohorts, cell-composition adjustment, and replication of the cross-tissue module structure.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents a conserved vascular-stromal contractile unit shared by brain and rectum. In this model, MYH11, MYLK, CALD1, CNN1, TPM2, and related proteins generate and regulate actomyosin force; FLNA, VCL, TLN1, FERMT2, ILK, PARVA, and TNS1 transmit that force through focal adhesions; and LAMA5, LAMB2, NID1, NID2, HSPG2, DCN, BGN, OGN, and PRELP provide the basement-membrane and extracellular-matrix scaffold. ATP2B4 could help tune contractile state through calcium handling, while SORBS1, SYNPO2, and related adaptors could coordinate cytoskeletal organization and migration. The shared signal may arise because comparable perivascular or smooth-muscle cells contribute to both tissues. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal pathway; testing it requires cell-resolved expression, protein localization, and perturbation of adhesion or contractility components.

The module should not be described as a newly discovered molecular complex or a new pathway. Absence of a STRING or hu.MAP relationship is not evidence of novelty. The appropriate conclusion is that the module-level tissue-pair association is a candidate observation requiring external validation, not an established new biological discovery.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects a shared cellular-state program in brain and rectum that couples immune or adhesion-related activity with preservation of RNA and protein quality. The strongest interpretation is a conserved intracellular protein-handling and gene-expression module centered on the CCT/TRiC chaperonin. The primary globally corrected evidence is the repeated enrichment of the chaperonin-containing T-complex and ATP-dependent protein-folding chaperone functions (GO:CC chaperonin-containing T-complex; q_global = 7.42e-05), (GO:MF ATP-dependent protein folding chaperone; q_global = 0.000312), (GO:MF protein folding chaperone; q_global = 0.00251), and (GO:CC protein folding chaperone complex; q_global = 0.00968). CCT3, CCT5, CCT7, and CCT8 form the clearest functional core. The same core is associated with RNA localization, telomerase-RNA localization, telomere maintenance, and regulation of DNA biosynthesis (23 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 7.42e-05–0.00489; see the table), but these terms should be interpreted cautiously because they are largely driven by the same four CCT genes and may reflect broad annotation of the chaperonin complex rather than a tissue-specific telomere program. **Cell composition.** This is not predominantly a plasma-protein or secreted-protein module: HPA identifies only one secreted gene, and the proteins detected in brain and rectum are mostly intracellular. Most genes have low tissue specificity, which supports a broadly expressed housekeeping-like interpretation rather than a program restricted to either brain or rectum. The module is also not strongly blood-cell specific overall. S100P is group enriched in several epithelial and blood-cell contexts, including granulocyte populations, while PDLIM1 has blood-cell enrichment and several genes show cell-type-enhanced expression in epithelial, reproductive, muscle, or trophoblast contexts. These observations make contamination by circulating plasma proteins unlikely, but they leave open the possibility that shared epithelial, stromal, vascular, or other common cellular components contribute to the cross-tissue co-abundance. The fact that protein detection is broadly present in both tissues is consistent with a real shared cellular program, although it does not establish that every module gene is co-expressed in the same cell.

### Novelty

The module should not be described as a newly discovered molecular complex. Its central components belong to well-established CCT, aminoacyl-tRNA-synthetase, translation, and cytoskeletal systems, with support from both STRING and hu.MAP (15 supplementary STRING terms; within-query FDR 0.00018–0.0015), (hu.MAP huMAP3_07938.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_06559.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09191.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_10656.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14584.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_01951.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_02637.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_13688.1). A potentially novel aspect is the reproducible co-abundance of this combination across brain and rectum, particularly the linkage between the CCT core, RNA-processing genes, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, and epithelial or cytoskeletal genes. However, absence of some edges from STRING or hu.MAP does not demonstrate novelty, and co-abundance alone does not prove direct interaction. The appropriate conclusion is that the cross-tissue module organization is a candidate novel systems-level observation, while its principal molecular mechanisms are largely established.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: a broadly expressed proteostasis and biosynthetic state links the brain and rectum module. In this model, the CCT/TRiC core folds actin, tubulin, and other newly synthesized proteins; the aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase group supports accurate translation; DHX9 and UPF1 connect RNA processing, transport, and surveillance; and FLNB, TMOD3, PDLIM1, and TWF1 connect proteostasis to cytoskeletal organization. EIF6 and BRIX1 may represent a ribosome-biogenesis or translational interface, while ACLY may couple the state to cytosolic biosynthetic metabolism. The observed co-abundance could therefore arise from coordinated maintenance of protein synthesis, RNA quality control, and cell architecture in shared or similarly stressed cell populations. This is a mechanistic hypothesis, not a demonstrated causal pathway; direct validation would require cell-resolved measurements, perturbation of the CCT core, and testing whether the peripheral genes change concordantly.

Database incompleteness, shared cellular composition, and broadly expressed housekeeping-like biology are alternative explanations that require validation in independent cohorts, cell-resolved data, and perturbation experiments.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module represents a conserved cellular maintenance state in which mitochondrial carbon and nitrogen metabolism supplies reducing power, amino-acid intermediates, folate-derived one-carbon units, and nucleotide precursors, while ER translocation, glycosylation, disulfide processing, and vesicle export maintain protein quality and trafficking. Nuclear RNA-processing and DNA-repair components may couple this metabolic and proteostasis state to biosynthetic demand and genome maintenance. The observed co-abundance could therefore arise from coordinated response to metabolic or proteostatic demand in shared cell populations of brain and rectum, rather than from a dedicated brain–rectum pathway. This is a mechanistic hypothesis; the lack of globally significant GO/KEGG enrichment means it should not be treated as demonstrated pathway-level enrichment.

### Novelty

The biochemical interpretation is not a new discovery: the complex-I relationships, oxidative-phosphorylation functions, and disease associations are already documented by STRING, hu.MAP, MyGene.info, Open Targets, and the literature annotations in the fact sheet. However, the conserved co-abundance module across brain and rectum may still represent a useful systems-level observation, especially because it combines a complex-I core with IMMT, FLOT1, and AGRN. That cross-tissue co-abundance pattern should be described as a potentially novel module-level finding rather than a newly discovered molecular interaction. Absence of FLOT1 or AGRN from the reported STRING and hu.MAP relationships does not establish novelty, because database coverage, evidence thresholds, tissue context, and the distinction between co-abundance and physical interaction can all explain the absence. A defensible novelty claim would require comparison with prior cross-tissue co-expression studies and independent replication in additional cohorts or datasets.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects coordinated regulation of mitochondrial complex-I abundance and inner-membrane organization in metabolically demanding cells shared by brain and rectum. The complex-I core could support NADH-to-ubiquinone electron transfer and proton-gradient formation, while IMMT could link the signal to cristae architecture. FLOT1 may represent membrane-domain or vesicle-trafficking coupling, and AGRN may represent a cell-surface or extracellular-matrix component associated with tissue or cellular composition rather than a direct member of complex I. Under this model, variation in the module would track respiratory capacity and possibly oxidative-stress susceptibility, but the current evidence does not establish causal regulation, direct physical interaction of every member, or disease-specific activity. The hypothesis should be tested by measuring complex-I protein abundance and activity, mitochondrial membrane organization, cellular composition, and AGRN or FLOT1 expression in matched brain and rectum samples.

The module is most convincingly a nuclear DNA replication, replication-fork, and DNA-damage-management module. Its central functional signature is the MCM helicase and replicative licensing machinery: MCM2, MCM4, MCM5, MCM6, and MCM7 jointly support the MCM and CMG complexes and recur throughout the globally corrected replication, helicase, chromosome, recombination, and DNA-repair terms (47 globally significant GO/KEGG terms, q_global 1.32e-06–0.0446; see the table). **Cell composition.** The module is overwhelmingly composed of intracellular, predominantly nuclear proteins rather than plasma or secreted proteins. ### Novelty

The biological mechanism represented by the module is not a new discovery: the MCM complex, CMG-associated replication machinery, CDK1 cell-cycle control, MSH2 mismatch repair, and XRCC6 DNA-end repair are established biology, and many of these relationships are already represented in STRING and hu.MAP (hu.MAP huMAP3_05529.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14758.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_08554.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_11832.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_12180.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_06061.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_07113.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_11119.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09466.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_10862.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_14306.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09860.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_00154.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_08848.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_02602.1) (hu.MAP huMAP3_09238.1). The module should therefore not be claimed as a newly discovered molecular complex or pathway. A potentially novel aspect is the conserved co-abundance organization of this established genome-maintenance program across brain and rectum, especially the precise combination with RNA-processing, chromatin, nuclear-import, translation, membrane, and stress-related components. However, the FACT SHEET does not establish novelty of that cross-tissue association: absence of a relationship from STRING or hu.MAP would not prove novelty, and the existing interaction and complex evidence argues for a conservative interpretation. The strongest defensible conclusion is that this is a potentially informative conserved tissue-pair co-abundance module built from largely known biology, not evidence by itself for a new pathway.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects a shared abundance program marking cells that are licensed for DNA replication or responding to replication-associated stress in both brain and rectum. The MCM core may provide the replication-fork scaffold, CDK1 may couple the program to cell-cycle progression, and MSH2 plus XRCC6 may provide mismatch, recombination, and double-strand-break surveillance. HNRNPK, PTBP1, SRRT, KPNA2, ANP32E, and RPRD1B could connect this state to RNA processing, nuclear transport, chromatin regulation, and transcriptional control, while HYOU1 and TMEM214 could reflect stress or endoplasmic-reticulum-associated responses. ANXA2 and CLIC1 may represent a membrane or cytoskeletal interface and could also contribute to sample or cell-state composition. This model predicts that the module will be strongest in proliferative, progenitor-like, regenerative, or DNA-stressed cell populations, but co-abundance alone does not establish direct regulation, physical interaction for every pair, or a causal brain-rectum mechanism.

### Novelty

This is unlikely to be a newly discovered molecular function because its central relationships and complexes are already represented in STRING and hu.MAP, and the principal genes have established roles in splicing, translation, ribonucleoprotein assembly, and nuclear transport. The potentially novel aspect is the coordinated co-abundance of these components as one module shared by brain and rectum, especially the cross-tissue association between spliceosomal, translation, signal-recognition-particle, and nuclear-pore features. That claim should be treated as a systems-level observation rather than proof of a new physical complex or a tissue-specific mechanism. Absence of database links for some module pairs does not establish novelty, since database coverage and experiment types are incomplete.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the shared module represents a conserved cellular state in which nuclear RNA processing, spliceosome assembly, nucleocytoplasmic RNA transport, translation initiation, and cotranslational targeting are coordinately regulated. In this model, spliceosomal factors such as SF3B3, SNRNP200, DDX46, and SNRPA1 support productive pre-mRNA maturation; NUP155 and NUP160 support export and import through the nuclear pore; EIF3C, EIF3L, and EIF4G1 support translation initiation; and SRP68 with SRP72 couples selected nascent proteins to endoplasmic-reticulum targeting. This is a mechanistic hypothesis inferred from co-abundance and known annotations, not evidence that all members physically interact or that the module drives a disease phenotype. It can be tested by examining coordinated changes in RNA splicing, nuclear export, translation, and secretory-pathway output in matched brain and rectum samples.
