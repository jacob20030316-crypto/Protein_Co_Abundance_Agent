# Conserved co-abundance modules: rectum and testis

## Module 0

**Tissue pair**: rectum and testis  
**Genes (25)**: SLC3A2, MTHFD1, CANX, ERAP1, EWSR1, FAM120A, FUS, HNRNPK, HNRNPL, EIF5, IQGAP1, KRT18, KRT8, MYH14, OCIAD1, ALDH18A1, JUP, PTBP1, PTBP3, SFXN1, SRP72, STAT1, TFRC, VPS35, XRCC6

### Function

The best-supported interpretation is a broadly expressed RNA- and nucleic-acid-associated cellular module. RNA binding is supported by (GO:MF RNA binding; q_global = 0.00441), with substantial representation of HNRNPK, HNRNPL, PTBP1, PTBP3, FUS, EWSR1, FAM120A, EIF5, SRP72, and related genes. Nucleic-acid binding is independently supported by (GO:MF nucleic acid binding; q_global = 0.00805), which also includes STAT1 and XRCC6. The module therefore appears to combine RNA processing and alternative splicing with gene-expression regulation, translation-related activity, DNA repair, and intracellular protein trafficking. The central network structure is consistent with an RNA-binding ribonucleoprotein core rather than a narrowly tissue-specific pathway. The keratin pair and junctional genes add an epithelial structural component, while SRP72, CANX, VPS35, SLC3A2, and TFRC connect the module to secretory-pathway targeting, protein quality control, membrane transport, endosomal recycling, amino-acid transport, and iron uptake. The GO terms establish the broad molecular function, but they do not by themselves prove that all of these processes operate as one physical complex.

**Cell composition.** The module is not dominated by plasma or secreted proteins and is not strongly blood-cell specific. HPA indicates generally low tissue specificity for most genes, low immune-cell specificity for most genes, and only a small secreted-to-blood component represented by TFRC and ERAP1. Protein detection is broadly present in both rectum and testis, supporting the possibility that the shared signal reflects common cellular machinery rather than a tissue-restricted secreted factor. The HPA pattern includes enhanced or enriched signals in several epithelial, reproductive, metabolic, and immune-related cell types, including colonocytes, goblet cells, spermatogenic cells, trophoblast-related cells, and some myeloid populations. Thus, the module may partly reflect shared epithelial or proliferative cell states and broadly expressed housekeeping-like functions. The data do not support interpreting it primarily as a blood contamination module, although cellular admixture cannot be excluded.

### Novelty

This module should not be called a wholly new molecular complex. Its central hnRNP and PTBP relationships, the FUS-EWSR1-HNRNPK connections, the PTBP1 and PTBP3 complex context, and the KRT8-KRT18 pair are already represented in STRING or hu.MAP. The potentially novel aspect is the conserved co-abundance of these established components with transport, metabolic, endosomal, epithelial, and DNA-repair genes across rectum and testis. However, the fact sheet does not provide an external comparison with previously reported rectum-testis modules, replication cohorts, perturbation data, or tissue-deconvolution analyses. Therefore, the defensible conclusion is that this is a potentially interesting tissue-pair co-abundance configuration built from largely known cellular machinery, not a demonstrated new pathway or complex.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the module reflects a shared cellular state in rectum and testis in which RNA processing and post-transcriptional regulation are coordinated with protein synthesis, endoplasmic-reticulum quality control, membrane and endosomal trafficking, nutrient and iron acquisition, and maintenance of genomic integrity. HNRNPK, HNRNPL, PTBP1, FUS, and PTBP3 could organize or mark an RNA-processing program; SRP72 and CANX could couple that program to secretory-protein targeting and folding; SLC3A2 and TFRC could indicate nutrient and iron demands; VPS35 could support receptor recycling; and XRCC6 could mark a need for DNA double-strand-break repair. The epithelial keratin and junctional component may indicate that this state is especially visible in epithelial or structurally active cells. This remains a hypothesis because co-abundance does not establish directionality, physical binding, cell autonomy, or causal regulation.

## Module 1

**Tissue pair**: rectum and testis  
**Genes (5)**: A1BG, SERPINC1, CP, HPX, KNG1

### Function

The module is best interpreted as a circulating plasma-protein module associated with blood microparticles. The only GO/KEGG result surviving global correction is (GO:CC blood microparticle; q_global = 0.000696), in which every module gene overlaps the blood-microparticle term. Its members collectively point to complementary plasma functions: coagulation regulation through SERPINC1 and KNG1, heme transport and oxidative-stress protection through HPX, copper and iron handling through CP, and a plasma glycoprotein of less-defined function through A1BG. The shared co-abundance signal therefore most directly reflects coordinated abundance of extracellular blood proteins rather than a demonstrated rectum-specific or testis-specific intracellular pathway. The absence of additional globally corrected GO/KEGG terms should be stated explicitly: no other GO/KEGG term passed global correction. Supplementary STRING terms reinforce plasma, cerebrospinal-fluid, bone-marrow-cell and blood-microparticle associations (4 supplementary STRING terms; within-query FDR 8.52e-07–0.00014), but they are not additional globally corrected enrichment results.

**Cell composition.** All module members are HPA tissue-enriched in liver, all are secreted to blood, and none is detected by HPA protein measurements in rectum or testis. The module is not blood-cell enriched in the HPA summary; the genes are not detected in immune cells. This combination strongly favors a plasma or blood-microparticle origin, possible vascular carryover, or shared extracellular material in the samples rather than local production by rectum or testis. HPA also links several members to hepatocytes, while CP has enrichment in choroid-plexus epithelial and other epithelial cell types and A1BG has nervous-system and epithelial cell-type associations. Those associations do not overcome the consistent liver and secreted-to-blood pattern.

### Novelty

The conserved rectum–testis co-abundance observation may be useful as a tissue-pair finding, but the underlying biological theme is not convincingly new. The module is entirely composed of liver-enriched secreted plasma proteins, its central relationships are represented in STRING, and its members repeatedly occur together in hu.MAP plasma-protein complexes. The globally significant blood-microparticle enrichment (GO:CC blood microparticle; q_global = 0.000696) and the supplementary plasma-related STRING results (15 supplementary STRING terms; within-query FDR 8.52e-07–0.0015) provide a coherent established explanation. Absence of a particular edge or complex would not by itself establish novelty, and the supplied evidence does not demonstrate a new molecular mechanism. A potentially novel aspect is the reproducible conservation of this plasma-protein abundance pattern across rectum and testis, but that should be tested against blood contamination, vascular content and sample-composition effects before being called a tissue-specific discovery.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: this conserved module represents coordinated delivery or retention of liver-derived plasma proteins in blood microparticles or extracellular plasma material present in both tissue sample types. In this model, SERPINC1 and KNG1 contribute coagulation and kallikrein–kinin regulation, HPX and CP provide heme, iron, copper and oxidant-handling capacity, and A1BG is a co-abundant plasma glycoprotein whose functional contribution is less defined. The observed tissue-pair conservation would then arise from shared exposure to the circulation or shared extracellular compartments rather than synchronized expression by rectal and testicular cells. This hypothesis predicts that the module should track vascular or plasma content and should not require local protein detection in either tissue.

### Novelty

The biological theme is not new: focal adhesions, integrin-linked junctions, actin remodeling, and smooth-muscle or sarcomeric contractility are established functions of many module genes. The module is therefore better described as a potentially novel co-abundance configuration or cross-tissue observation rather than a newly discovered molecular pathway. Its potentially original aspect is the coordinated preservation of this structural program across rectum and testis, including genes with different tissue and cell-type preferences. That claim remains provisional because the fact sheet does not provide an external catalog of previously reported rectum-testis modules or an independent replication cohort. The presence of known STRING and hu.MAP relationships argues against calling the whole module unprecedented. Conversely, the absence of some genes from STRING or hu.MAP does not prove novelty; it may reflect incomplete databases, context dependence, or limited experimental coverage.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: the shared module reflects a conserved contractile-adhesion unit in smooth-muscle-like, vascular, peritubular-myoid, myoepithelial, or related stromal cells present in both tissues. In this model, integrin-linked adhesion is organized by ITGB1, FERMT2, TLN1, PARVA, and VCL; FLNA and related filamin proteins couple membrane-associated adhesion machinery to actin networks; and the MYLK-CALD1-CNN1 axis tunes actomyosin contractility. CFL2, DSTN, GSN, and associated proteins could remodel actin turnover, while FLNC, CSRP1, PDLIM3, and CRYAB could support muscle or sarcomeric architecture. Coordinated abundance of these components could therefore report shared cell composition and mechanical-state regulation rather than a tissue-specific rectum or testis biochemical pathway. This mechanism is a hypothesis and requires cell-resolved expression, protein localization, perturbation, and replication in independent samples.

### Novelty

The module-level co-abundance observation may be novel, because the specific conserved rectum-testis grouping and its network structure are not demonstrated by the supplied STRING or hu.MAP annotations. However, it should not be called a newly discovered molecular pathway. Many component functions and several relationships are already established, including COPII and Golgi transport involving SEC23A and USO1, COPI or Golgi organization involving COPG2 and COG1, vesicle sorting involving AP3D1, and SNARE-related trafficking involving NBAS, SCFD1, and STXBP2. The limited direct STRING connectivity also argues against claiming that the entire module is a known physical complex. A defensible conclusion is that the conserved co-abundance module is a potentially new systems-level association assembled from largely known cellular machinery. Its novelty would require replication in independent cohorts, control for cell composition, and comparison with published tissue co-expression modules.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: rectal and testicular cells share a quantitatively coordinated membrane-trafficking state in which ER export, Golgi organization, vesicle sorting, endosomal recycling, exocytic docking, and cytoskeletal remodeling are co-regulated. SEC23A and USO1 could support ER-to-Golgi delivery, COG1 and COPG2 could maintain Golgi retrieval and organization, AP3D1 and SNX4 could sort cargo, and NBAS, SCFD1, and STXBP2 could support tethering or SNARE-dependent fusion. CTTN, DNM2, DBNL, TJP2, EXOC5, and WASHC4 could connect trafficking to actin, junctions, endocytosis, and plasma-membrane delivery. The mitochondrial, peroxisomal, and stress-related genes may reflect the energetic and proteostatic demands of this trafficking state, or may represent secondary co-abundance. This hypothesis predicts coordinated changes in cargo transport and organelle morphology, but the co-abundance data alone do not establish directionality, cell autonomy, or physical assembly.

### Novelty

The module may be a new tissue-pair-level co-abundance observation, but it should not be described as a newly discovered molecular complex or pathway. Its central signals correspond closely to established spliceosomal machinery, the MCM replication machinery, RNA helicases, RNA export, and RNA surveillance or processing components, with direct support from STRING, hu.MAP, and gene annotations. The novel aspect could therefore lie in the coordinated abundance of these systems across rectum and testis, or in the particular tissue-pair context, rather than in the individual gene functions or physical interactions. Absence of hu.MAP or STRING support for peripheral members such as CNOT1, DHX9, CORO7, or several trafficking-related genes does not prove novelty; it may reflect incomplete interaction coverage, indirect regulation, or co-abundance without direct binding. The module is best considered a potentially novel systems-level association built around well-established intracellular machinery, requiring replication in independent cohorts and cell-composition-aware analyses before claiming tissue-specific novelty.

**Hypothesis.** Hypothesis: in both rectum and testis, a shared population of transcriptionally active or cycling cells coordinately increases nuclear RNA maturation and genome-maintenance capacity. The spliceosomal and RNA-processing arm, including SF3B3, EFTUD2, SNRNP200, DDX46, DDX42, DHX9, ADAR, and related factors, could support rapid processing, remodeling, editing, export, and quality control of transcripts. In parallel, the MCM arm could support replication licensing and replication-fork activity, while PRKDC could connect the program to DNA-damage repair. The observed module may therefore reflect a coupled RNA-processing–replication state rather than direct physical interaction among every member. A competing explanation is shared cell composition, particularly proliferative germ-cell or progenitor-like states, and this should be tested by cell-type deconvolution, within-cell-type analysis, and comparison with matched non-proliferative tissue populations.
